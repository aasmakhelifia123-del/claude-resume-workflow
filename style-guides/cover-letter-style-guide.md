# Cover Letter Style Guide

This guide captures voice, framing, and editorial preferences for writing
cover letters. It's prompt context for Claude — not read by the build —
so edit it freely to encode your own preferences.

A **`## Personalize`** section at the bottom holds the bits unique to you.

---

## What a cover letter is for

The cover letter's job is to **make the hiring manager want to read the
resume carefully.** It is a narrative, not a summary. If it reads like a
paragraph-form paraphrase of the resume, it has failed.

A good cover letter does three things:

1. **Shows the reader you understand what this specific role needs** —
   not just "I'm qualified," but "I get what you're actually trying to
   solve."
2. **Gives the reader one concrete, specific, memorable thing about you**
   — a story, a moment, a piece of work that sticks. The thing they'll
   remember when they pick up the phone.
3. **Leaves them curious to learn more**, not exhausted by the recap.

---

## Voice and tone

**Aim for:**

- Confident without being a brag-list. Stories carry confidence better
  than adjectives do.
- Specific over general. Name the tool, the problem, the moment.
- Warm but not performative. Skip "I am incredibly excited about this
  opportunity" — actual interest shows through the specificity of what
  draws you to the role.

**Avoid:**

- Opening with "I'm writing to apply for…" — every cover letter opens
  this way, so none of them do their job in the first sentence.
- Paragraph-form resume paraphrasing.
- Naming the previous employer in cover-letter prose. Refer to them as
  "my last role" or "my previous role." The resume names them; the letter
  doesn't need to.
- Overt enthusiasm statements ("I'd be thrilled to…", "I'm incredibly
  passionate about…"). Show enthusiasm through engagement with what the
  company actually does.
- Apologetic or gap-calling language. **Never** call out what you haven't
  done, what you're weak on, or what might be missing. The resume and the
  conversation are where gaps get discussed.
- Generic closer lines ("I look forward to hearing from you"). Close with
  something that lands.
- Em-dash density. A few well-placed em-dashes are fine; using them as
  default sentence-breaks makes the prose feel mannered.

## Calibration — important

Most people **downplay their accomplishments** — and that matters even
more in a cover letter than a resume, because a cover letter is a
confidence medium.

- Stories should be told with the confidence they deserve. If you were
  the driver on something, frame yourself as the driver — not a humble
  sidekick.
- Don't hedge specific achievements with softening phrases like "I was
  fortunate to," "I had the opportunity to," "I was able to help with."
  Say what you did.
- If there's tension between humble framing and a compelling story, pick
  compelling every time. The cover letter is where you advocate for
  yourself.

**The line that doesn't move:** don't invent anything. Stories must come
from the story bank or from context you've shared. But between
"technically true but modest" and "technically true and bold," always
pick bold.

## Structure

A cover letter is **3–4 short paragraphs, half a page to 2/3 of a page
max.** Anything longer and no one reads it. Recommended structure:

### Paragraph 1 — The hook

Open with something that shows you've thought about *this company* and
*this role.* Not "I'm applying for" but something specific — a reference
to what the company does, the problem the role is solving, or a framing
that shows you understand the shape of the work. End the paragraph with
why you're a natural fit, in one sentence.

### Paragraph 2 — The story

This is the load-bearing paragraph. Pick **one** story from your
[`story-bank.md`](story-bank.md) that best matches what the JD is asking
for and tell it concretely. Not "I have experience with X" — actually tell
the short version of the story. Situation, what you did, what happened as
a result. Specific enough that the reader can picture it.

### Paragraph 3 — The bridge

Tie the story to the role. Not a recap ("as you can see from the above…"),
but a forward-looking bridge: *this is the kind of work I want to keep
doing, and it's what I think I'd bring to your team.* This is where you
can reference one or two other strengths briefly, but don't list — weave.

### Paragraph 4 (optional) — The close

Keep it short. Something warm and curious. Not a begging-for-an-interview
line. A good close acknowledges the reader's time and leaves a door open
naturally.

Avoid corporate-cringe closers like "Happy to talk through what the first
ninety days could look like." Prefer warmer, curiosity-forward closes
that point at the work: *"Curious to hear more about what you're
building — would be glad to chat."*

## Story selection

Your story bank should contain headline stories and shorter detail
stories. When selecting:

- **Match the story to the JD's central concern.** A role about building
  from scratch wants a build-from-scratch story; a role about incident
  response wants an incident story; a role about cross-layer thinking
  wants a story that crosses layers.
- **Prefer stories with a specific moment or detail** over ones that are
  mostly summary. Details make stories stick.
- **The story should imply skills, not state them.** A good story about
  owning a migration demonstrates project ownership without having to say
  "I have project ownership experience."

---

## Format

- Plain prose, no bullet points, no headers.
- Greeting: "Hi [Name]," if you have a hiring manager's name; "Hi
  [Company] team," if you don't. Avoid "Hello" (stilted) and "To Whom It
  May Concern" (archaic). "Dear" is fine for unusually formal companies
  but defaults to "Hi" otherwise.
- Sign off with "Best," or "Warmly," followed by your name. "Sincerely"
  is fine but slightly stiff for most target companies.
